product title

open source multi symbol stock order book simulator for indian markets

describe your product

This project is an open-source stock order book and trading simulator for Indian exchanges. It will run a matching engine that accepts basic order types like market and limit orders and keeps a price-time priority book for each symbol. Trades are generated whenever compatible buy and sell orders cross. Around this core we plan a simple interface where users submit simulated orders, see live depth, and replay sessions so they can watch how liquidity moves at different prices. Configuration options will let users adjust lot size, tick size, and trading hours to approximate Indian rules in a simplified way. The whole stack will be released under the MIT license, so colleges, small brokers, and startups can host and modify it without vendor lock-in.

why the team has chosen this product

The reason we chose this product is that order books are at the center of modern markets in India, and in India, these engines are always proprietary and invisible to students or small groups of people. We are BCA students interested in software engineering and basic finance, and this project offered the possibility of working on something that impacts real economic infrastructure but is within our scope of a learning project. The matching process makes us think of data structures, concurrency, and fairness, and this connects to our course material in algorithms and operating systems. Reading about different types of orders and risk checks makes us think of SEBI documents and Indian exchange manuals rather than sticking to our textbooks. There is also a need to fill in the space of Indian education tools, and most simulators of Indian markets are broker-specific and closed-source. A source engine that others can view and change makes sense to fill this space and act as a foundation for later batches or nearby colleges.

what problems it solves

Currently, Indian exchanges use closed software for running the exchanges, and students mostly get to see price charts through broker apps. They cannot see the book when many orders arrive at once or when large cancellations occur. This puts a distance between the relatively simple theory and the realities that algorithmic thinking cares about. Our simulator attempts to close this distance by providing a transparent book where all actions are visible and traceable. A second problem is that existing tools for paper trading are broker-specific and do not allow modification of matching logic and data structures. With our MIT-licensed engine, groups of people can experiment with different book structures; they can also compute various microstructure metrics like spread and order imbalance on Indian exchange data.

potential users and target audience

The intended users that we envision include Indian students taking BCA, engineering, MBA, and finance classes who want to grasp how electronic order-driven markets really work. Another group could include faculty members who teach data structures, operating systems, or financial market classes. They could use the simulator as part of their classes and could ask students to add a new kind of order or measure how latency changes as the data structure changes. Another group could include fintech companies or small brokerages who need a safe place to experiment before connecting to live exchanges. Our open source version can run on modest hardware, so we can use that as a sandbox for them. Another group could include retail investors or communities who want to grasp how liquidity and impact cost work for Indian securities, which could use our system for workshops. Another group could include researchers who want to study Indian market microstructure, who could use our system to run controlled experiments.

innovations in this project

One such innovation is to implement the matching engine as modular pieces, making it easy to replace the data structure for the order book from a list to a more efficient tree data structure, all without altering the underlying UI code. This makes it easier to learn about the relative efficiency and complexity of this approach compared to the Indian model of order flow. Another innovation is to implement multiple symbols early on, rather than making this an afterthought later on. A single instance of the matching engine will maintain multiple equities or contracts, keeping their state separate, making this more representative of a small exchange or multi-asset exchange in India. Another aspect to consider is to ensure that all matching decisions are traceable, so a log of all events is kept, including time stamps and symbols, and also to allow for the export of this data for further study. This also provides a basis for learning about price, time priority, and other rules, as well as providing a starting point for studying alternatives such as batch auctions and simple auctions, which are also employed in India. Finally, making the stack available under the MIT license and documenting the cost model is an innovation in accessibility since current engines that understand the Indian market are locked inside the vendor or the exchange and cannot be inspected.

13 how it will differ from existing solutions

Existing tools that contact Indian markets can be illustrated by three examples, NSE paathshaala is a virtual trading platform from the national stock exchange that lets users place mock orders on live like Indian data, but it is offered as a hosted service and does not expose its matching engine or internal order book implementation to students, moneybhai by moneycontrol is a stock market game where players get virtual cash such as one crore rupees to trade Indian shares mutual funds and other assets using real market data, it is useful for learning basic investing, but again users only see the front end and portfolio not the detailed book or matching rules, a third example is tradingview paper trading which many Indian traders use with local exchange data to practice strategies on advanced charts, that tool focuses on charting and order entry while the execution logic stays inside a closed platform and the user does not control the engine design, our project differs by putting the matching engine and order book at the center and releasing their source code under a MIT license so that Indian students and startups can inspect modify and benchmark the core logic itself. It will be tuned for local constraints like Indian trading hours and common lot patterns and even in simplified form will support multiple symbols by default so that a small universe of securities can be simulated. We plan to compare our simulator qualitatively with platforms like NSE paathshaala, moneybhai and tradingview paper trading in terms of how much depth of book they reveal, what control they give over rules and how easily they integrate with custom analytics, the aim is not to replace these established services but to offer a transparent and modifiable alternative that fits classroom use and early experimentation in the Indian context.

14 what makes it challenging enough yet feasible in four months

The problem is complex because even a simplified version of an order book has many subtle cases to deal with. We must ensure that price time priority is maintained and that the internal state is never inconsistent. The addition of multi-symbol support and interface usability is also a burden because we must think about how to store information, how to keep latency reasonable, and how to make it easy for users to understand the depth chart without becoming confused. However, I think that if we define a tight but solid core scope for this project, it should be achievable in four months. We can make it complex enough to be interesting, yet not so complex that a student team can't manage it.

15 how success and quality will be measured

Our criteria for a successful project include technical criteria, functional criteria, and user criteria so that the project is not considered successful only based on the final demo. From a technical point of view, we will write test cases to test common and tricky order sequences to check that matches and that the remaining quantities follow the rules. We will also test the basic throughput of the local machine and check that the support of multiple symbols does not corrupt the data. From a user point of view, we will ask a few of our classmates and at least one faculty member to perform simple experiments with our simulator, and short feedback forms will be used to ask about the clarity of the interface, usability, and whether the simulator helped them understand order book behavior. If most of the test cases pass and feedback is cautiously positive, we consider our project a success and think of areas to be improved in the future.

16 resources required and feasibility

The resources we need are modest, such as development laptops with standard programming tools, a repository on GitHub, and perhaps a small cloud server for hosting tests and demos. We are working with open-source languages and libraries, so there are no costs related to licenses. To create realistic behavior, we might use publicly available descriptions of Indian market rules and any available historical data for free or through institutional licenses to inform our synthetic order flows. If detailed data is hard to obtain, we might use patterns generated to resemble common statistics, such as busy opening hours and quiet mid-session periods. The resources we need are meager, and this is a realistic plan.

19 evidence literature and research support

The studies on market microstructure in Indian markets and other emerging economies highlight the significance of transparent and fair order-driven systems in terms of liquidity and price discovery; however, these papers and reports published by SEBI and Indian exchanges highlight the importance of order books, auctions, and risk management in terms of growing participation of retail and institutional investors in these markets; yet these papers assume the availability of effective trading engines; moreover, these papers do not offer open models that can be accessed by students or small organizations. There are open-source models available globally for simulating trading engines and order-matching engines; these models can be effective tools in classrooms/laboratories; these models can be used to experiment with different order types, fee structures, and auction mechanisms without risking money. Discount brokers, API-based trading, and mobile-based trading have become increasingly popular in the Indian market; these have also led to growing interest in algorithmic trading and automation among young investors; yet most of the learning happens on black boxes provided by brokers. A MIT license-based Indian market simulator can provide an opportunity to have access to the code/rules; yet the environment can remain safe. It may support academic projects, hackathons, or even training for people entering the Fintech or market infrastructure industry, by matching the design with the public’s current regulation guidance and microstructure research, we hope the chances of increasing the overall understanding rather than proposing an alternative exchange are high, even if the chances of adoption are low, the project may serve as an example in the debate about openness in the Indian capital markets, in the long run, there is the possibility of having a pool of students who have worked with such engines, thereby adding value to the overall brokers, vendors, or exchanges in the country, thereby adding value to the overall economy, this is yet another reason why we think the idea is backed by reality in the context of the Indian scenario, more background information about the topics above may be found in the public domain in the SEBI documentations at https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/commondocs/ch6_p.pdf.

20 risk analysis and mitigation

The key risks involved are underestimating the complexity of the engine, spreading ourselves too thinly across features, and problems of coordination within our team. Additionally, unless ordering life cycle logic is well planned, we may encounter subtle bugs that show up late and are hard to trace. To minimize this, we plan to write small design notes before we code, and we should also write tests for typical and edge case scenarios early on. Another potential problem is scope creep, where features such as live data feeds, complex order types, etc., are tempting but may consume a lot of time. We also anticipate maintaining a list of must-have and nice-to-have features, which we'll review on a weekly basis under the guidance of our faculty.

The team-related potential risks are those of uneven contribution, where one particular individual might dominate a particular module. To address this, we plan to share code reviews, check in regularly, and ensure that decisions are well documented so that someone else could pick up when needed.

ethics privacy moral and legal issues

While the simulator does not actually process real money, there are ethical, privacy, and legal issues that we should be concerned with. We should clearly indicate that it is not meant to be used as a tool for investments. We should also clearly indicate that no trades will be sent to the current exchanges. We should also ensure that, if we were to use real historical or current market data, we do not distribute it in any way that goes against the terms of the exchanges. Morally, we should not represent it as a way to make quick profits from trading. We can represent it as a way to understand the risks involved. If someone wants to make it so that the simulator can be used to trade, they will need to study the SEBI regulations, the connectivity regulations, etc. We will ensure that the simulator is strictly in the sandbox mode. We will also ensure that the code is clearly documented so that others can reassess the legal issues when using the code.

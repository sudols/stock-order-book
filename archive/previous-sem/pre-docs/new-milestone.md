# design thinking and innovation project milestone 1 bca

## product title

IndiaBook: stock order book sandbox

## describe your product

This project is an open-source stock order book and trading simulator for Indian exchanges. It will run a matching engine that accepts basic order types like market and limit orders and keeps a price-time priority book for each symbol. Trades are generated whenever compatible buy and sell orders cross. Around this core we plan a simple interface where users submit simulated orders, see live depth, and replay sessions so they can watch how liquidity moves at different prices. Configuration options will let users adjust lot size, tick size, and trading hours to approximate Indian rules in a simplified way. The whole stack will be released under the MIT license, so colleges, small brokers, and startups can host and modify it without vendor lock-in.

## why the team has chosen this product

We selected this product because order books sit at the heart of modern markets in India, yet in India the engines are almost always proprietary and invisible to students or small teams. As BCA students interested in software engineering and basic finance, we saw a chance to work on something that touches real economic infrastructure but still fits a learning project. The matching logic makes us think about data structures, concurrency, and fairness, which connects to what we study in algorithms and operating systems. Reading about order types and risk checks pushes us to look at SEBI documents and Indian exchange manuals rather than staying only with textbooks. There is also a gap in local education tools since most simulators for Indian markets are broker-specific or closed. An open-source engine that others can inspect and modify seems like a reasonable way to reduce that gap and create a base that later batches or nearby colleges can reuse.

## what problems it solves

Today, Indian stock exchanges are operated using closed software, and students mostly see only price charts through broker apps. They cannot see how the book changes when many orders hit together or when big cancellations happen. This creates a distance between simple theory and the details that matter for algorithmic thinking. Our simulator aims to close some of that distance by giving a transparent order book where every insert, match, and cancel is visible and can be replayed. Another issue is that existing paper trading tools are tied to brokers and do not let users change matching rules or data structures. With an MIT-licensed engine, small teams can experiment with alternative book designs; they can also compute microstructure metrics like spread and order imbalance on Indian-style data. This gives more room for research and teaching without depending fully on commercial vendors.

## potential users and target audience

The main users we imagine are Indian students in BCA, engineering, MBA, and finance courses who want to understand how electronic order-driven markets actually function. Faculty members who teach data structures, operating systems, or financial markets can also use the simulator in labs and assignments; they could ask students to add a new order type or to measure how latency changes when the data structure is changed. Fintech startups and small brokerages form another group; they often need a safe place to test user flows or risk checks before connecting to live exchanges. Our open engine can run on modest hardware, so it can serve as a sandbox for them. Retail investors and community groups who wish to see how liquidity and impact cost behave in Indian securities may also use it for workshops. Finally, researchers who study Indian market microstructure can adapt the engine to run controlled experiments, though they may extend it for more precise work.

## innovations in this project

One innovation is to design the matching engine in modular pieces so that the order book data structure can be swapped from a simple list to a more efficient tree without changing the UI logic. This lets learners compare performance and complexity under Indian-style order flows. A second innovation is to support multiple symbols from early in the design rather than treating that as an afterthought. A single engine instance will maintain books for several equities or contracts while keeping their states separate. That brings the simulator closer to how a small exchange or multi-asset venue in India behaves. We also want every matching decision to be reproducible, so the engine will log events with time stamps and identifiers and allow export of full histories for analysis. This helps students verify that price, time priority and other rules work as described and gives a basis for testing alternative mechanisms like batch auctions or simple auctions used in some Indian contexts. Finally, licensing the stack under MIT and documenting low-cost deployment is itself a form of innovation in accessibility because current engines that understand Indian markets are usually locked inside vendors and exchanges and cannot be inspected.

## 13 how it will differ from existing solutions

Existing tools that contact Indian markets can be illustrated by three examples, NSE paathshaala is a virtual trading platform from the national stock exchange that lets users place mock orders on live like Indian data, but it is offered as a hosted service and does not expose its matching engine or internal order book implementation to students, moneybhai by moneycontrol is a stock market game where players get virtual cash such as one crore rupees to trade Indian shares mutual funds and other assets using real market data, it is useful for learning basic investing, but again users only see the front end and portfolio not the detailed book or matching rules, a third example is tradingview paper trading which many Indian traders use with local exchange data to practice strategies on advanced charts, that tool focuses on charting and order entry while the execution logic stays inside a closed platform and the user does not control the engine design, our project differs by putting the matching engine and order book at the center and releasing their source code under a MIT license so that Indian students and startups can inspect modify and benchmark the core logic itself, it will be tuned for local constraints like Indian trading hours and common lot patterns and even in simplified form will support multiple symbols by default so that a small universe of securities can be simulated, we plan to compare our simulator qualitatively with platforms like NSE paathshaala, moneybhai and tradingview paper trading in terms of how much depth of book they reveal, what control they give over rules and how easily they integrate with custom analytics, the aim is not to replace these established services but to offer a transparent and modifiable alternative that fits classroom use and early experimentation in the Indian context.

## 14 what makes it challenging enough yet feasible in four months

The project is challenging because even a simplified order book has many edge cases around partial fills, cancellations, and rapid sequences of orders. We have to make sure that price time priority holds and that the internal state never becomes inconsistent. Adding multi-symbol support and a usable interface adds further load since we must think about how data is stored, how latency stays reasonable, and how users can read the depth without confusion. However, the task seems feasible in four months if we fix a narrow but solid core scope; we can restrict ourselves to a few order types, modest performance targets, and a focused front end. This keeps the work larger than a small assignment yet still within what a student team can complete with steady effort.

## 15 how success and quality will be measured

We plan to judge success by technical, functional, and user dimensions so that it is not based only on a final demo. Technically, we will prepare test cases that cover common and tricky order sequences and check that matches, and the remaining quantities always follow the documented rules. We will also measure basic throughput on local machines and confirm that multi-symbol support does not corrupt data. We expect users to be able to place and cancel orders, view depth and trade history for each symbol, replay a session and export logs for analysis. On the user side, we will invite a small group of classmates and at least one faculty member to run simple experiments, short feedback forms will ask about clarity of the interface, ease of use and whether the simulator helped them understand order book behavior. If most core tests pass and feedback is cautiously positive we will treat the first version as successful while noting areas for later improvement.

## 16 resources required and feasibility

The resources we need are modest, development laptops with regular programming tools, a shared repository on GitHub, and possibly a small cloud or on-premises server for hosting tests and demos. We will rely on open-source languages and libraries, so there are no extra license fees. For realistic behavior, we may use public descriptions of Indian market rules and any freely available or institutionally licensed historical data to shape synthetic order flows. If detailed datasets are hard to obtain, we can start with generated patterns that mimic common statistics like busy opening periods and quieter mid-sessions. Overall, the required software and hardware appear achievable within normal college access, which makes the plan realistic.

## 18 short term and long term planning

For the first two weeks, we plan to study reference materials on Indian order-driven markets, review the project brief and fix the scope and technology stack. In weeks three and four, we will implement a minimal single-symbol engine with a clean order life cycle and start building a command line or very simple web interface. By the middle of the semester, the target is to have an end-to-end flow for one symbol including logging and basic tests. In the next block of time, we will extend the design to support multiple symbols, refactor data structures if needed and, parallel work on the user interface will gradually move from simple tables to clearer visual layouts. The final month will focus on hardening, more tests, writing user, developer documentation and running small trials with classmates or faculty, looking beyond this milestone we outline long term tasks such as adding pre-open auctions, basic circuit breaker behavior, additional asset classes and broker test API, these future items form a roadmap that later teams or contributors can pick up while our batch remains responsible for a stable educational core.

## 19 evidence literature and research support

Research on market microstructure in India and other emerging economies points to the importance of transparent and fair order driven systems for liquidity and price discovery, papers and reports from SEBI and Indian exchanges discuss how order books, auctions and risk controls support growing participation by retail and institutional investors, however these documents usually assume the presence of robust trading engines, and they rarely provide open models that students or small organizations can inspect. International experience with open source trading simulators and matching engines shows that simplified but faithful replicas can be useful in classrooms and labs, they allow experimentation with order types, fee structures and auction formats without risking actual money. In India, the rise of discount brokers, API based trading and mobile investing has increased interest in algorithms and automation among young investors, yet most learning still happens on top of black box broker platforms. A MIT licensed Indian focused simulator can offer a middle ground where the code and rules are visible, but the environment stays safe. It can support academic projects, hackathons and introductory training for people joining Fintech or market infrastructure firms, by aligning the design with public regulatory guidance and microstructure findings while keeping the implementation open, we hope to increase practical understanding rather than propose a competing exchange, even if adoption remains limited the project can act as a concrete reference point in discussions about openness and capacity building in Indian capital markets, over time, a pool of students who have worked with such engines can contribute more confidently to local brokers, vendors or exchanges which supports the broader economy. They bring both coding skills and a clearer sense of how regulations, fairness and performance interact in real systems. This link between education, open tooling and market readiness is one more reason we believe the idea has practical backing in Indian conditions, more background on these topics can be found for example in public SEBI documentations at https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/commondocs/ch6_p.pdf.

## 20 risk analysis and mitigation

Key risks include underestimating engine complexity, spreading effort too thinly across features, and facing coordination issues within the team. If order life cycle logic is not carefully planned, we may run into subtle bugs that appear late and are hard to trace. To reduce this, we will write small design notes before coding and create tests for typical and edge case scenarios early, scope creep is another concern because topics like live data feeds, or complex order types are attractive but can easily consume time, we expect to maintain a list of must-have and nice to have features and review it weekly with faculty guidance. Team related risks include, uneven contribution or dependence on one person for critical modules. We aim to mitigate them through shared code reviews, regular check ins and documenting decisions so that others can step in when needed.

## ethics privacy moral and legal issues

Although the simulator does not handle real money, there are ethical, privacy, and legal questions we should respect. We must state clearly in the interface and documentation that it is an educational tool and not an investment platform and that no orders are routed to current Indian exchanges. If we ever use real historical or delayed market data, we should check the licensing terms of exchanges or vendors and avoid redistributing datasets in ways that violate agreements. When logging user activity for research or teaching, we should collect minimal personal information, store it securely, and inform users about what is captured. Morally, we should avoid presenting the simulator as a quick path to trading profits instead, we can frame it as a way to understand risks and mechanics. If at some future stage someone wants to connect the engine to live trading they would need to study SEBI rules, exchange connectivity norms and investor protection guidelines carefully. Within this project, we will keep the system strictly in a sandbox mode and will document third party components so that others can reassess compliance when they reuse the code.
